How sound travels

Notes
  • Sound travels through air. You can feel air pushing or pulling things (like the wind!)
  • It travels through air the same way waves travel in a pond when we throw a stone.
  • It travels even if air doesn't "move": you can test this by doing a wave (like in a stadium). The wave travels through a group, but people don't move.
  • When this wave of air "pushes and pulls" very quickly in our ear, a tiny tiny amount, and thousands of times a second, we hear sounds.
  • Try it out: You can feel this as a vibration on your throat while you speak, our while you hum different notes
  • Also: put your hand on a speaker, and hear (feel!) different sounds. Does the pitch make a difference on how the vibration feels?

How computers hear

Notes
  • For people: sound is what our ears detect from air "wiggling" very fast.
  • For a computer: sound is when a microphone or a speaker vibrates. When something vibrates, it wiggles a tiny bit, moving up and down.
  • A computer's (or phone's) microphone will measure how much movement that vibration creates, and it will store a number.
  • You can't measure this with a ruler! The movement is so tiny that you can't see it with your eyes.
  • But we can cheat and "see" the measurements a computer stores by using a program.

    • Sound is just numbers

    Notes
  • A computer will "remember" a list of numbers that make up sound. This can be a list of numbers that makes music, or a list of numbers that records how you speak. It's all just numbers. A very very very long list of numbers: a computer will remember 48000 numbers for every second of audio it records!
  • Because it is all numbers, a computer can change how things sound. For example, we can make things sound louder by multiplying every number in this list by 2. If you are a computer, it's important to know your times tables.
  • We can do more than just see the volume: we know that the higher the number is, the louder it sounds. We can also hear what happens when the numbers "wiggle" faster or slower.
  • How quickly air wiggles determines the pitch, but this is very hard to see directly when we speak, because our pitch changes very quickly. We can get a computer to play a simpler sound, that will change the pitch only when we tell it to. This way, we can see how the speed of wiggling affects the sound.
  • Experiment: Record a few real sounds with Audacity. See how speaking louder changes amplitude. Try it with different sounds, too. Zooming in we can see the individual numbers that make up sound for a computer.

    • Your hearing range

    Notes

    Our hearing is very sensitive and we can hear very low pitches, and very high pitches, but every person hears a little bit differently. This also depends on how old you are, and how much you've cared for your hearing health.

    Experiment 4: Check hearing range, verify test speaker can reach 15K+. If test speaker isn't too broken, children should be able to hear much higher pitches than me.

    Your ear is full of little hairs that "dance" with sound. Like this one: youtube.com/watch?v=pij8a8aNpWQ

    As you get older, or if you listen to very loud sounds very often, some of those hairs stop dancing. Make sure you take care of your hearing health!

    Colour

    Notes

    Different people will hear different ranges of pitches. Animals too: cats and dogs can hear pitches 4 times higher than humans. Dolphins and bats up to 10 times more.

    A similar thing happens with colour: you can see colours in the rainbow, but bees can see colours that we can't see. Their eyes work differently.

    In fact, sound pitch and light colours are very similar: they are both how fast something "wiggles" (sound needs air to travel through, but light doesn't need anything!). For computers, both colours and sounds are just numbers, so we can use a program to convert "sound-pitch" to "light-pitch" (colour!)

    Experiment 5: Adjust the tone to see how it affects colours: the higher the frequency, the higher the pitch, and the closer it should get to the violet end of the rainbow.

    Speed of sound

    Notes

    Because to computers everything is just numbers, we can convert one type of numbers to another: we can convert a list of sound-numbers to electricity-numbers or to light-numbers, and send those to another computer, so that the other computer can make sound.

    Why do we do this? Sounds don't travel very far, and they don't travel very fast.

    Experiment 6: Measuring the speed of sound; combine two recording channels to show that distance is proportional to flight-of-time delay.

    Sound travels about 350 meters per second. Sound would take a full second to travel between the school and the playground in Highbury Fields. It would take about 4 minutes to travel between London and Brighton.

    That seems fast, but imagine speaking to someone who will take 8 minutes to answer? And imagine how much you'll have to shout!

    Light is a lot faster; it takes about 1 second to travel from the moon to us, so phones use light (or electricity, which is just as fast) to send each other lists of numbers.

    The shape of sound

    Notes

    When a computer receives audio and plays that for us, we can recognize who is talking, and we can also recognize music and melodies. How do we do that? Each sound has a shape.

    Open the "spectrogram" (this is a fancy way of saying "drawing of how frequency changes with time"). Make just one sound, one phoneme, into the microphone, and see how your voice is made up not of one single pitch, but many different pitches, in a complex pattern that changes with time.

    See how vowels like "O" or "A" have a lot of low pitch, and consonants like "S" or "T" are mostly high pitch. Also try musical instruments. Can you see the different notes?
